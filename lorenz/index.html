Underlying Theory: The Lorenz System

History & Atmospheric Convection

In 1963, meteorologist and mathematician Edward Lorenz developed a simplified mathematical model for atmospheric convection. He was attempting to predict weather patterns by mathematically modeling how air is heated by the earth, rises, cools, and falls. Instead of predictable weather forecasting, Lorenz stumbled upon something revolutionary: his system demonstrated what is now famously known as deterministic chaos.

The Mathematics of Chaos

The Lorenz system is defined by three coupled, non-linear ordinary differential equations:

dx/dt = σ(y - x) dy/dt = x(ρ - z) - y dz/dt = xy - βz
  • x: Rate of convective overturning.
  • y: Horizontal temperature variation.
  • z: Vertical temperature variation.
  • σ (Prandtl number): Ratio of fluid momentum to thermal diffusivity.
  • ρ (Rayleigh number): Proportional to the temperature difference between the top and bottom of the fluid.
  • β: Geometric ratio of the convection cell.

Numerical Integration

Because these equations are non-linear, they cannot be solved analytically (there is no simple formula for the exact answer). Instead, this application uses a 4th-Order Runge-Kutta (RK4) numerical integration method. RK4 computes the slope at four different points within a time step (dt) and takes a weighted average to calculate the next position. This provides a much higher "finesse" and stability compared to simpler methods like Euler integration, preventing numerical errors from spiraling out of control in highly chaotic trajectories.

Sensitive Dependence (The Butterfly Effect)

The most profound discovery Lorenz made is that for certain parameter values (like ρ = 28), two completely identical systems starting from points that differ by an infinitesimally small amount (e.g., 0.0001) will quickly diverge and trace entirely different paths. This "sensitive dependence on initial conditions" was coined the Butterfly Effect. Despite this chaos, the trajectory never flies off to infinity; it remains trapped on a 3D manifold known as a Strange Attractor, taking the visually striking shape of a butterfly.

Guidance for Exploration

Try adjusting the Rayleigh number (ρ) to see the system transition between states:

  • ρ < 1: The origin (0,0,0) is a stable attractor. Convection dies out.
  • 1 < ρ < 24.74: The system settles into two stable fixed points (steady convection rolls). Watch the line spiral inward!
  • ρ = 28 (Default): The classic chaotic regime. The trajectory jumps unpredictably between the two lobes.
  • ρ > 90: Highly chaotic regimes, interspersed with windows of stable periodic orbits (e.g., at ρ ≈ 99.96).
